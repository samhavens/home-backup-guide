Begin with your needs

Each of you may have a different concern: spoiled food, a hot bedroom, losing touch with family, or another thing to maintain. Start by hearing both answers.

What has been difficult in past outages?

List what stopped working, how long the outage lasted, and what caused the most discomfort.

What must keep working?

Separate essentials from normal whole-house use. Include any necessary medical equipment.

Could you stay somewhere cool?

For a long, hot outage, consider a place to stay and transportation. Include plans for pets or health needs.

Could either of you manage it alone?

Automatic operation, clear instructions, and dependable service can be worth paying for.

What cost keeps your savings comfortable?

Choose an affordable limit before comparing major installations.

Why these answers come before equipment

A few hours of backup requires much less stored energy than several days at home. Your own outages matter more than a general description of Calabasas.

A low-priced system is a poor fit if it requires lifting, troubleshooting, or maintenance you do not want to do. A larger system may be worthwhile if staying home through a long outage is important to you.

Solar’s financial value also depends on your actual bills, roof condition, and expected time in the house. Sunshine alone does not establish that it is a good purchase.

Make safe cooling part of the plan

Keeping food cold matters. Having a comfortable, safely cooled place to spend time and sleep may matter more.

Older adults are more vulnerable to heat. Do not rely on a fan as your main cooling source in very hot weather. CDC guidance on heat and older adults.

Ask an adviser to compare three levels

  1. Essentials, with somewhere cool to go. Back up selected appliances and communications, with a practical plan to leave when needed.
  2. Essentials plus one cooled room. Explore a room where both of you can comfortably spend time and sleep.
  3. Normal cooling throughout the house. Compare the added capacity and cost if this is important to you.

One suitable cooled room deserves a serious look before paying to back up all central air conditioning.

Plan for smoke and reduce cooling demand

The room needs suitable cooling equipment and enough backup power. Its size, installation constraints, and measured electricity use determine feasibility.

Plan air filtration and cooling together. Some single-hose portable air conditioners can pull more smoke into the home. An adviser should evaluate the proposed equipment for smoky conditions. EPA guidance for a cleaner-air room.

Ask whether shading the sunniest windows or other modest improvements would slow the house’s heating. Reducing cooling demand may be more useful than buying a larger battery.

Agree in advance on when to leave for your alternative place to stay. Backup equipment does not replace evacuation instructions or a dependable plan for dangerous heat.

Your choices and likely costs

These screening budgets exclude incentives. Obtain a quote for your house before deciding. The options provide very different amounts of power and runtime.

Plug-in battery

$700-$1,200

Illustrative 1-2 kWh unit, before accessories or setup

A small purchase for selected appliances if your needs are modest and you have a workable plan for heat or a longer outage.

Check appliance operation and setup

It supplies the appliances connected to it. It does not automatically back up other house circuits.

Check whether it starts each appliance and runs it long enough. Ask about automatic transfer, what happens when the battery empties, and whether the appliance restarts when utility power returns.

Confirm who will set it up. Both of you should be able to use it without unwelcome lifting or cable changes.

Wired home battery

$12,000-$20,000

Illustrative 10-15 kWh system, without new solar

Quiet, automatic backup for selected circuits. It charges from utility power and avoids a rooftop solar project.

Check circuits and battery capacity

Ask which circuits remain powered and how long they run at the everyday reserve setting. During an outage, the battery has a finite supply unless another compatible source can recharge it.

This is a useful comparison point: it shows what quiet, automatic backup costs on its own. Example manufacturer guidance on batteries without solar.

Solar + home battery

$30,000-$50,000

Illustrative 6-10 kW solar + 10-15 kWh storage

Consider it when the roof is suitable, conservative bill savings support the added cost, and recharging during outages is valuable to you.

Solar’s additional benefits

Solar may replenish storage during an outage when production and compatible equipment allow. Sunlight and sufficient production are not guaranteed.

Compare its additional cost with additional savings relative to an adequate battery-only system. Include roof work and future panel removal and reinstallation costs.

Standby generator

$15,000-$25,000

Illustrative permanent system around 20 kW

Consider it for substantial electricity needs through prolonged outages, with an acceptable noise level and a dependable fuel and service arrangement.

Check fuel and ongoing service

Automatic starting does not eliminate upkeep. Ask who handles checks during long outages, how service works during a widespread outage, and what it costs.

Hear the proposed model under a meaningful electrical load, rather than only during its quieter exercise cycle. Assess the location relative to bedrooms and neighbors.

Obtain a complete installation scope covering gas capacity and electrical work. The installer should also specify permits and zoning clearance for the proposed location.

Generator + home battery

$38,000-$54,000

Advertised local hybrid packages; configuration varies

Consider this added complexity only if you need extended generation and quieter periods with the engine off.

Compatibility needs a written answer

Ask whether the generator can actually recharge the proposed battery for another quiet night. Coexisting with a generator does not always mean charging from it.

For example, Tesla states that Powerwall cannot charge directly from a generator. Tesla’s generator compatibility explanation.

Where the budgets come from

The supplied assessment uses manufacturer listings for small batteries, EnergySage quote data of about $14,567 for 13.5 kWh of storage in California and $23,890 for 9.59 kW of solar in Calabasas, and local installer advertisements for generator packages.

Treat these amounts as planning estimates. The original small-battery listings and generator advertisements were not identified by URL in the supplied material. Major roof work, electrical-service changes, or difficult gas installation can materially change costs.

kW measures how much power equipment can supply at once. kWh measures stored energy. Being able to start an appliance does not mean being able to run it all night.

Ask how long it will last

“Backs up the house” is too vague. A quote should name the appliances, describe the conditions, and state how long they will run.

The reserve setting matters

A battery used for everyday bill savings may be partly discharged when an unexpected outage begins. Holding more energy in reserve improves readiness, but leaves less available for daily savings.

The backup estimate and savings estimate must use compatible assumptions. The same stored energy cannot be fully available for both purposes at once. Manufacturer explanation of backup reserve.

  • How long will your named essentials run from the normal reserve setting?
  • What if the outage starts after sunset?
  • What changes when cooling is running?
  • What if there is little solar production for an extended period?
  • How will you know when to reduce use or leave for your alternative place to stay?

Keep your savings comfortable

Decide what you can spend while preserving the reserves you want available. Include continuing bills and repairs as well as the purchase price.

Backup can be worth buying for comfort even when it does not produce a financial return. Evaluate solar’s extra savings and outage-recharging benefit separately.

Set your affordability limit
  • How much cash can you spend comfortably?
  • What monthly expense is manageable?
  • What unexpected repair could you absorb?
  • How long do you expect to benefit from improvements at this house?

Retirement does not automatically rule out a long-term investment. Projected future bill savings also do not replace cash you may need sooner.

Compare the extra cost of solar

Suppose an adequate wired battery costs $15,000, and solar brings the project to $35,000. The additional cost is $20,000.

If solar adds $1,800 in annual savings compared with the battery alone, simple payback is about 11 years. At $900 a year, it is about 22 years.

These are illustrations. They exclude financing and maintenance. They also leave out the value of keeping cash available. Solar’s ability to recharge during outages also has value.

Check financing and conservative savings

Request the cash price and financed price for identical equipment. Compare total payments and any future increases alongside the interest rate or monthly payment.

Some solar loans include substantial markups above the cash price. CFPB report on solar financing.

For a lease or power-purchase agreement, get written answers about payment increases, maintenance, battery replacement, and moving or ending the agreement.

Compare purchase cost and remaining utility bills over the time you expect to own the home. Include maintenance and financing. Ask for a stress test with no assumed electricity-price increases and lower solar production. California’s solar consumer protection guide.

If the project puts uncomfortable pressure on your savings, reconsider its size and purpose.

Choose something easy to live with

Before buying, both of you should understand what happens during an outage, what you must do, and who will help if the equipment fails.

Noise and pollution

A fuel generator produces engine noise and local exhaust. Batteries have no combustion exhaust at the house, although manufacturing and charging electricity have environmental impacts.

Maintenance

Get a written service plan. For a generator, name who will handle required checks and shutdowns during extended operation.

Roof protection

Assess the roof first. Specify responsibility for waterproofing and roof damage. Ask what panel removal and reinstallation will cost for later roofing work.

Installation and handover

An experienced independent electrical contractor is an option. Ask for relevant experience, a complete permitted scope, and dependable ongoing service.

Require a supervised backup demonstration, instructions you both understand, and one clearly identified service contact.

Useful details for contractor conversations

A California C-10 electrical contractor may perform solar electrical work. Judge the firm’s experience with solar and batteries, roof coordination, and service arrangements.

Generator service intervals depend on the model. Ask the installer to show you the manufacturer’s written requirements for continuous operation and explain who will carry them out.

If the roof is nearing replacement, address that before installing panels. CPUC guidance on choosing and owning solar.

Check financial help early

Eligibility could change the affordable choice. Being retired alone does not establish eligibility. Do not subtract a rebate until the program confirms you qualify and funding is available.

Solar-paired storage rebate

Clean Power Alliance offers a $2,000 base rebate, with qualifying additions up to $3,500 total. It is for eligible solar-paired batteries. Do not assume it applies to a standalone battery.

Check the CPA Sun Storage rebate

Medical-related assistance

If either of you has qualifying medical needs, ask SCE about Medical Baseline and backup-battery assistance. Medical Baseline has no income requirement.

Check SCE Medical Baseline

Portable batteries and permanent-battery pilots

SCE’s Critical Care Backup Battery program provides a portable battery at no cost, including delivery and setup with training for eligible customers with qualifying medical-device needs in high-fire-risk areas.

Check the Critical Care Backup Battery program

SCE’s 2026 plan also describes a $7,500 permanent-home-battery rebate pilot and a fully subsidized route for certain income-qualified customers. Conditions include Medical Baseline enrollment and repeated outages, with other requirements.

The plan describes five or more outages since January 1, 2023. Ask SCE which outages count, whether applications are open, and whether funding remains. This plan is not confirmation of a benefit for your address.

Read SCE’s 2026 assistance plan (PDF)

Federal tax credit and rebate details

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit is unavailable for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Do not include a 30% homeowner credit in a new project’s budget. IRS residential clean energy credit rules.

CPA’s additions are $1,250 for a qualifying Reliability+ address and $250 for qualifying medical-baseline or income eligibility, up to $3,500 combined. The $2,000 base incentive applies to new reservation or claim applications submitted on or after September 1, 2026.

Bring these to the conversation

Your electricity bills, a short account of recent outages, and a comfortable spending limit.

Ask these four questions

  1. What is an affordable way to keep our essentials working during the outages we actually have?
  2. What would it cost to keep one suitable room comfortably cool too?
  3. What would rooftop solar add for our electricity use? Does it suit our roof and budget?
  4. What would we personally have to do during an outage, and who would help if the equipment failed?
Take this checklist to each installer
  • Compare essentials-only backup with a design including suitable cooling.
  • Named circuits and equipment, including air-conditioning startup power.
  • Runtime at the usual reserve setting, with load and sunlight assumptions.
  • An after-sunset outage scenario and an extended low-sunlight scenario.
  • Complete installation price, including required roof or utility work and permits.
  • Annual savings using your actual electricity use and conservative assumptions.
  • Cash and financed prices for identical equipment.
  • Service costs, warranty, repair responsibilities, and a backup demonstration.

Choose equipment you can use comfortably and afford throughout ownership.
